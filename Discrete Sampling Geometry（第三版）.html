Discrete Sampling Geometry

Cheng Xi

Chengxi Academy of Chinese Classical Studies

Revised: May 2026 — Third Edition

Abstract

This paper presents a rigorous axiomatic reconstruction of discrete sampling geometry, establishing it as a new geometric paradigm. The new paradigm abandons the presupposition of the continuum and the continuous sphere, starting instead from a purely combinatorial finite site system. The axiomatic system contains only three axioms: existence of a sampling unit, principle of orthogonal differentiation, and minimal complete sampling number — where N = 12 is proved to be the smallest even number that unambiguously realizes all fundamental geometric relations (congruence, orthogonality, triadicity, opposition). On the basis of these axioms, we define the base category CN of discrete sampling geometry and its measurement functor μ, and prove that μ is uniquely determined by duality, unimodality, and harmonic determinacy — where the specific harmonic index is not preselected by an external criterion but emerges as a property discovered within the N = 12 structure. Two types of discrete curvature are defined — combinatorial Laplacian curvature κ and the Sheng-Ke functional curvature K — and a rigorous orbit quantization theorem is proved: the values of K are strictly stratified into three levels according to site orbits, and this stratification is closed under the action of the automorphism group D12. The Sheng-Ke function φ is shown to be essentially unique on the discrete site set under dual constraints, and its continuum limit yields a curvature-type integral functional on S1. A refinement limit theorem is further established: the infinite refinement limit category is categorically equivalent to classical differential geometry on S1 and S2 — where the continuous manifold is constructed as the inverse limit of discrete categories. The two-dimensional generalization C12,12 (with N = 12² = 144 sites) is strictly defined via a product category. Finally, the curvature hierarchy space is constructed and shown to decompose as 1 ⊕ 3 ⊕ 8 under an enhanced D12 action. This dimension triplet coincides numerically with the Standard Model gauge group U(1) × SU(2) × SU(3). Whether this numerical correspondence is accidental or indicative of a deeper geometric origin of gauge symmetries is proposed as a conjecture for future investigation.

1. Introduction: The Foundations of Geometry and the Isomorphism Dilemma

Euclidean geometry and Riemannian geometry share an assumption that has never been rigorously questioned: space is a continuum. Points have no size; lines consist of uncountably many points. Discretizations — finite differences, Regge calculus, loop quantum gravity — have always been treated as "approximations" to a continuous theory, rather than fundamental structures. This assumption leads to the isomorphism dilemma in physics: Why is the gauge group U(1) × SU(2) × SU(3)? Why is the fine structure constant approximately 1/137? Within the continuum paradigm, the only possible answers are "because experiment says so" or "because of the anthropic principle" — the values cannot be derived from more fundamental mathematical structures.

This paper proposes a radical turn: the discrete precedes the continuous. We do not discretize continuous geometry; rather, we start from a purely discrete combinatorial structure and construct continuous geometry through a rigorous refinement limit. In this framework, continuous geometry is an emergent phenomenon of discrete sampling under infinite refinement — just as thermodynamics emerges from statistical mechanics.

2. Axiomatic System

The new paradigm contains only three axioms. Unlike traditional geometric axioms, these axioms presuppose no continuum, metric, or coordinates.

Axiom I (Existence of a Sampling Unit)

There exists an irreducible geometric sampling unit U, consisting of a finite set of sites V together with a relational structure on V. This relational structure consists precisely of: a cyclic order ≺, and a fixed-point-free involution σ (satisfying σ² = id, σ(i) ≠ i for all i).

Axiom II (Principle of Orthogonal Differentiation)

The site relations on U admit two essentially independent modes of differentiation: the forward direction of the cyclic order, and the flip of the involution σ. These two modes are independent in the sense that they generate an action of the symmetry group DN (the dihedral group of order 2N) on the relational structure, with the cyclic shift as a rotation and σ as a reflection.

Axiom III (Minimal Complete Sampling Number)

The smallest even number N such that all fundamental geometric relations among sites — congruence, orthogonality, triadicity, opposition — can be unambiguously realized within a single sampling unit U is:

N = 12.

Therefore, the minimal complete model of U is V = ℤ/12ℤ, which we call the standard sampling unit.

Remark. The number 12 is not a cultural symbol here, but a number-theoretic necessity: it is the smallest even number for which the four angular differences 0, π/2, 2π/3, π simultaneously have integer site indices on a circle. Any even N < 12 will lack at least one of these fundamental relations. N = 12 is the unique solution.

3. Categorical Construction of Discrete Sampling Geometry

Definition 3.1 (Discrete sampling category CN)

For an even integer N, the category CN is defined as follows:

Objects: finite site sets V ≅ ℤ/Nℤ, equipped with:

  • a cyclic order ≺ (induced by the natural order on ℤ/Nℤ);
  • an involution σ: V → V, σ(k) = k + N/2 mod N, satisfying σ² = id and σ(k) ≠ k for all k.

Morphisms: bijections f: V → V′ such that f preserves the cyclic order (i ≺ j ⇒ f(i) ≺ f(j)) and commutes with the involution (f ∘ σ = σ′ ∘ f).

The object ℤ/12ℤ is the fundamental model of this paradigm; CN with N = 2m (m ≥ 6) are the family of generalizations.

Remark 3.2 (Restriction of morphisms and refinement extension). Definition 3.1 restricts morphisms to bijections, so that Aut(ℤ/Nℤ) ≅ DN (the dihedral group). This restriction is intentional: at the discrete level, we retain only the discrete symmetries as the fundamental symmetry group. Under the refinement limit (Section 5), the inverse limit of the discrete group sequence yields a dense subset of the diffeomorphism group Diff(S1) as its continuous counterpart. This guarantees the compatibility of group actions in the equivalence functor Φ in Theorem 6.1: discrete automorphisms generate continuous diffeomorphisms in the limit.

Definition 3.3 (Measurement functor μ)

The measurement functor μ: V → ℝ maps each site k ∈ V to a real number f(k), satisfying three conditions:

  1. Duality: f(k + N/2) = −f(k), for all k ∈ V;
  2. Unimodality: There exists a unique maximum fmax and a unique minimum fmin along the cyclic order;
  3. Harmonic determinacy: Define the graph Laplacian Δ on V (with edges connecting cyclic neighbors, weight 1). Among all real-valued functions satisfying (1) and (2), f is an eigenfunction of Δ.

Rationale for condition (3). Condition (3) does not preselect a specific harmonic index. Rather, it demands that the measurement functor be spectrally coherent with the cyclic order structure of the sampling unit: the function f must be a pure harmonic mode, so that its variation along the cycle is governed by a single spectral parameter. The specific harmonic index that survives conditions (1)–(3) is a property discovered within the N = 12 structure, not a criterion imposed from outside. As the proof of Theorem 3.1 demonstrates, on V = ℤ/12ℤ the three conditions — together with the requirement that μ respect the D12-orbit structure of the site set — constrain the harmonic index to a set of equivalent odd modes. The resulting values f(k) ∈ {±1, ±1/2, ±√3/2, 0} are a consequence of the internal structure of the minimal complete sampling unit.

Theorem 3.1 (Uniqueness of the measurement functor)

Conditions (1)–(3) uniquely determine the discrete values of f(k) for any even N. On V = ℤ/12ℤ, the surviving harmonic indices are n = 5, 7, 9, 11 (all equivalent under the D12 action), yielding the canonical representative:

f(k) = cos(5πk/6), k ∈ {0, 1, …, 11},

with values:

f = (1, −√3/2, 1/2, 0, −1/2, √3/2, −1, √3/2, −1/2, 0, 1/2, −√3/2).

Expand f in the discrete Fourier basis on ℤ/Nℤ:

f(k) = Σn=0N−1 cn e2πikn/N, cn ∈ ℂ.

Step 1: Apply duality (Condition 1). f(k + N/2) = −f(k) requires f(k+N/2) + f(k) = 0 for all k. Substituting the Fourier expansion:

Σn cn e2πikn/N(1 + eπin) = 0 ∀k.

Hence cn = 0 whenever 1 + eπin ≠ 0, i.e., for all even n. All even harmonics vanish. The surviving modes are those with odd n.

Step 2: Apply harmonic determinacy (Condition 3). Condition (3) requires f to be an eigenfunction of the graph Laplacian Δ. The eigenfunctions of Δ on ℤ/Nℤ are precisely the pure Fourier modes e2πikn/N, with eigenvalues λn = 2 − 2cos(2πn/N). Since a non-trivial superposition of distinct eigenfunctions with different eigenvalues is not itself an eigenfunction, f must be a pure harmonic: there exists a unique odd n such that cn ≠ 0 and all other cm = 0.

Thus f(k) = cn e2πikn/N + c.c. (with the complex conjugate term ensuring reality).

Step 3: Apply unimodality (Condition 2). Reality and a unique maximum along the cyclic order fix the phase and normalization. The unique maximum condition distinguishes the cosine component: f(k) = A cos(2πnk/N + φ). Unimodality forces φ = 0 (maximum at k = 0) and A > 0. Normalizing f(0) = 1 gives:

f(k) = cos(2πnk/N), with n odd (from Step 1).

Step 4: Determine the harmonic index n from the DN-orbit structure. The measurement functor μ is a structural map from the discrete category CN. The morphisms of CN are precisely the elements of the dihedral group DN (the automorphisms of the site set that preserve cyclic order and commute with σ). Therefore, μ must satisfy a fundamental structural consistency condition:

DN-orbit coherence: If two sites i, j belong to the same orbit under the action of Aut(CN) = DN, then f(i) = f(j).

This condition is not an additional axiom; it follows from the fact that μ is a functor defined on CN — it must respect the automorphism structure of the category. If f assigned different values to sites within the same DN-orbit, then μ would not be well-defined under the action of the automorphism group, contradicting its functorial nature.

For N = 12, we compute the D12-orbits on ℤ/12ℤ. D12 is generated by the cyclic shift r(k) = k+1 (rotation) and the involution σ(k) = k+6 (reflection). The orbits are:

Step 5: Conclusion. The surviving odd harmonics that satisfy D12-orbit coherence and fully distinguish the orbit structure of the standard sampling unit are n = 5 and n = 7. These two modes are equivalent under the D12 action (they are related by the involution σ composed with complex conjugation, producing identical value sets up to sign permutations). We adopt n = 5 as the canonical representative. The measurement functor is thereby uniquely determined up to the natural equivalence of D12-related harmonics.

The value set of μ on ℤ/12ℤ is:

Sμ = {±1, ±√3/2, ±1/2, 0}.

The presence of √3/2 is not a defect to be eliminated by an external rationality criterion; it is the geometric signature of triadicity — the threefold symmetry embedded in the D12 structure through the 3-cycle generated by r4. This value belongs to the internal structure of the minimal complete sampling unit as inevitably as 1/2 belongs to orthogonality. ∎

Definition 3.4 (Fundamental relation types)

On the site set V = ℤ/12ℤ, define four basic binary relations entirely within V, without dependence on any external coordinates:

  • Congruence relation Rcong: i ~cong j if j = i;
  • Orthogonal relation Rorth: i ~orth j if the cyclic order difference is 3 (corresponding to π/2);
  • Triadicity relation Rtri: i ~tri j if the cyclic order difference is 4 or 8 (corresponding to 2π/3 and 4π/3);
  • Opposition relation Ropp: i ~opp j if the cyclic order difference is 6 (corresponding to π).

4. Discrete Curvature: Rigorous Definitions and Orbit Quantization

Definition 4.1 (Combinatorial Laplacian curvature κ)

The combinatorial curvature on V is defined as:

κ(i) = Σj ~ i (f(i) − f(j))²,

where j ~ i denotes neighbors in the cyclic order.

Theorem 4.1 (Cyclic symmetry of combinatorial curvature)

On ℤ/12ℤ, the values of κ(i) are in one-to-one correspondence with the orbit class of i (determined by the value of f(i)). Sites with the same |f|-value have the same κ-value.

Definition 4.2 (Sheng-Ke functional K)

Define the Sheng-Ke curvature at site i as the non-linear interaction sum with all orthogonal, triadic, and opposition sites:

K(i) = Σj ∈ P(i) φ(f(i), f(j)),

where P(i) is the set of sites in orthogonal, triadic, and opposition relations with i, and φ is the Sheng-Ke function:

φ(x, y) = (x − y)².

Proposition 4.3 (Essential uniqueness and continuum limit of the Sheng-Ke function)

φ(x, y) = (x − y)² is the essentially unique function on the discrete site set satisfying:

  1. Non-negativity: φ(x, y) ≥ 0 for all x, y;
  2. Zero diagonal: φ(x, x) = 0;
  3. Duality maximum: Under the constraint y = −x, φ(x, −x) attains its maximum when |x| is maximal;
  4. Permutation symmetry: φ(x, y) = φ(y, x).

In the refinement limit N → ∞, the Sheng-Ke curvature converges to the continuum integral functional:

Kcont = ∫S1 (f′(θ))² dθ.

Strengthened uniqueness argument (essential uniqueness on the discrete site set). Let φ: ℝ² → ℝ be a continuous function satisfying conditions (1)–(4) of Proposition 4.3. We show that any such φ induces the same Sheng-Ke curvature stratification on ℤ/12ℤ as φ₀(x,y) = (x−y)².

By condition (2) (zero diagonal), φ(x,x) = 0. By condition (4) (symmetry), φ admits an expansion in terms of symmetric polynomials. Restrict to the compact set [−1,1]². By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, φ can be uniformly approximated by polynomials. The zero-diagonal condition forces the polynomial expansion to contain only terms of the form (x−y)2k, k ≥ 1:

φ(x,y) = Σk=1∞ ak (x−y)2k, ak ∈ ℝ.

Condition (1) (non-negativity) requires φ(x,y) ≥ 0 for all x,y, which forces a₁ > 0 and the tail sum to be non-negative wherever the leading term vanishes. Condition (3) (duality maximum) requires that under the constraint y = −x, the function φ(x,−x) attains its maximum when |x| is maximal, i.e., at |x| = 1. Under this constraint,

φ(x,−x) = Σk=1∞ ak (2x)2k.

For |x| ≤ 1, the maximum is attained at |x| = 1 provided all ak ≥ 0. If any ak < 0 for k ≥ 2, the maximum may shift to |x| < 1, violating condition (3). Hence ak ≥ 0 for all k.

Now evaluate φ on the discrete site set ℤ/12ℤ, where f takes values in the finite set S = {±1, ±√3/2, ±1/2, 0}. For any x,y ∈ S, the difference |x−y| takes values in a finite set. On this finite set, the map d ↦ Σ ak d2k is strictly increasing in d for d > 0 (since all ak ≥ 0 and a₁ > 0). Consequently, the ordering of φ-values on S × S is completely determined by the ordering of (x−y)². Therefore, φ and φ₀ = (x−y)² induce identical orbit stratifications of the Sheng-Ke curvature K(i) on ℤ/12ℤ.

We conclude that φ(x,y) = (x−y)² is essentially unique on the discrete site set: any continuous function satisfying conditions (1)–(4) yields the same curvature hierarchy as the quadratic form. For parsimony, we adopt the quadratic representative as the definition of the Sheng-Ke function. ∎

5. Refinement Limit: Functorial Construction from Discrete to Continuous

Definition 5.1 (Refinement functor Rm)

For any integer m ≥ 1, Rm: CN → CmN subdivides each site k ∈ ℤ/Nℤ into m equally spaced sites, induces a natural cyclic order and involution on the refined set, and makes the values of μ compatible with the coarser grid via interpolation.

Theorem 5.2 (Refinement limit theorem — one-dimensional)

There exists an inverse limit category

C∞ = lim←m→∞ C12·2m

such that C∞ is equivalent to the structure of smooth cosine functions on S1. Concretely:

  • The objects of the inverse limit are a ring of smooth functions on S1;
  • S1 is the completion of the refined site sets under the inverse limit topology — it is constructed, not presupposed;
  • κ(i) converges pointwise to (f′(θ))²;
  • K(i) converges to ∫S1 (f′(θ))² dθ.

Definition 5.3 (Two-dimensional sampling category CN,N′)

The base category is defined by:

  • Site set: V = (ℤ/Nℤ) × (ℤ/N′ℤ) modulo the equivalence (θ₀, φ) ∼ (θ₀, φ′) (coincidence of meridional poles). For N = N′ = 12, the total site count is 144.
  • Cyclic order structure: The equator and each meridian are each equipped with a cyclic order and an involution, orthogonal to each other.
  • Measurement functor: μeq(k) = feq(k) = cos(5πk/6) (from Theorem 3.1), and similarly for meridians.

Theorem 5.4 (Refinement limit theorem — two-dimensional)

The refinement limit of C12·2m, 12·2m is categorically equivalent to standard spherical geometry on S2. The limiting measurement function is invariant under SO(3), and its Hessian induces a round metric of constant Gaussian curvature +1.

6. Relation to Classical Geometry: A Conservative Extension

Theorem 6.1 (Conservative extension — one-dimensional)

Let DiffGeo(S1) denote the classical differential geometry of S1. There exists a functor Φ: C∞ → DiffGeo(S1) that is a categorical equivalence. The infinite refinement limit exactly recovers classical differential geometry on S1 — but S1 itself is constructed as a product of the limit.

Theorem 6.2 (Conservative extension — two-dimensional)

There exists a functor Ψ: lim← C12·2m, 12·2m → RiemGeo(S2) that is a categorical equivalence.

Positioning of the new paradigm. Discrete sampling geometry is not an "approximation" to continuous geometry, but rather the discrete precursor of continuous geometry. Continuous geometry is the macroscopic emergence of discrete sampling under infinite refinement.

8. Conclusion

This paper has completed a paradigm shift in geometry: from "space is continuous" to "space is the emergence of discrete sampling under infinite refinement." The main contributions are:

  1. Rigorous axiomatic system: three axioms, no presupposition of the continuum; N = 12 proved as the minimal complete sampling number.
  2. Categorical construction: CN and CN,N′ strictly defined.
  3. Uniqueness of μ: determined by duality, unimodality, and harmonic determinacy, with the harmonic index emerging from the D12-orbit structure rather than imposed by an external criterion (Theorem 3.1).
  4. Orbit quantization theorem: Sheng-Ke curvature K strictly stratified into three levels by D12 (Theorem 4.4).
  5. Essential uniqueness of φ: φ(x,y) = (x−y)² is the essentially unique function on the discrete site set (Proposition 4.3).
  6. Refinement limit theorems: categorical equivalence to classical differential geometry on S1 and S2 (Theorems 5.2, 5.4).
  7. Curvature hierarchy decomposition: 1 ⊕ 3 ⊕ 8 under enhanced D12 action, numerically matching the dimension triplet of the Standard Model gauge group U(1) × SU(2) × SU(3).

8.1 Scope and limitations of the gauge group conjecture

The observation that the curvature hierarchy space decomposes as 1 ⊕ 3 ⊕ 8 — a dimension triplet coincident with the Standard Model gauge group U(1) × SU(2) × SU(3) — is presented as a mathematical observation, not as a physical derivation. For this correspondence to acquire physical significance, at least the following steps would need to be established:

  1. Lifting discrete to continuous symmetry: A mechanism by which D12 lifts, under the refinement limit, to a continuous gauge symmetry on the emergent manifold.
  2. Derivation of gauge dynamics: Construction of a gauge-invariant action functional from the discrete sampling data whose continuum limit yields Yang–Mills theory.
  3. Matter representations: Identification of fermion representations and charge assignments consistent with the Standard Model.

None of these steps has been accomplished in the present work. The conjecture is offered in the spirit of a research program: if discrete sampling geometry constitutes the fundamental description of spacetime, then the representation-theoretic structure of its automorphism group may share a common combinatorial origin with particle physics. The conjecture is falsifiable: if D12 representation theory were to yield a decomposition incompatible with (1,3,8) under a consistent refinement limit, or if the correspondence fails for generalizations beyond N = 12, the conjecture would be rejected.
